GameFlow

A Project Management Platform for Game Development

A 1,000-person game studio where development coordination was scattered across group chats and shared cloud docs, leaving downstream teams to constantly miss upstream changes. We built a platform from 0 to 1 that pulls these workflows into one place. Now piloting in the core game departments.

Role
Product Design Intern
Company
Seasun Games
Timeline
Jun 2021 — Jun 2022
Scope
User Research · Product Design
01 · What Changed

Changes no longer travel through group chats, a status update now notifies the owner automatically.

PMs no longer check in with each group lead one by one, switching views shows the full picture.

Five departments went from five separate cloud docs to one shared view.

02 · My Role

Product Design Intern. Independently designed three core interactions: the change log's notification and traceability, the multi-dimensional filter panel's view switching, and the dual-axis progress toggle, all reviewed and approved by the PM. The underlying module architecture was led by the PM; early research and requirement prioritization were supporting roles.

03 · The Problem
Coordination That Couldn't Keep Up

The company runs 10+ live games with a team of over 1,000 people, requiring constant collaboration across Art, Programming, QA, and Operations. But the tools hadn't kept up: producers tracked progress in cloud spreadsheets, requirement changes traveled through group chats, and QA couldn't see upstream progress until the work landed in their queue.

This created three persistent problems: changes went unnoticed, the five teams each worked off their own view, and delays were always discovered after the fact. Our goal was to replace this fragmented coordination with a single platform.

04 · My Solution
The Form Follows the Problem

One platform, four forms. Requirements need to be found, so they live in a list. Stages need to be strung together, so they form a pipeline. Tasks need to be seen in motion, so they go on a kanban board. Data needs to be sliced on demand, so it sits behind a switchable panel. None of these forms was a given. Each was worked backward from one question: is this scenario about finding, moving, connecting, or switching? Answer that, and the right form follows.

1   Change Log

Changes Are Traceable and Searchable

The Problem

A requirement passes through multiple departments before it ships. When something changes mid-stream, downstream teams are often left in the dark, leading to wasted work.

What I Did

I designed the change log: once a status updates, the owner is notified automatically. No matter how many times something changes, anyone joining later can see the full history. The requirement pool itself was led by the PM; I contributed to the interaction design.

Why I Did It

Game development is iterative, not linear, and changes are frequent. So I made every change a traceable timeline rather than just showing the latest state, so downstream teams can see not only what it is now, but what changed along the way and why.

Design: Requirement pool list view
2   Multi-Dimensional Filter

One Panel, Switch on Demand

The Problem

The Art Center supports several projects at once, so leads struggle to track each member's workload; producers who want the full picture have to check in with each lead one by one.

What I Did

I designed a filter panel that switches between a "by project" and "by department" view, surfacing the needed data instantly. Connecting requirements, development, testing, and release into one pipeline was led by the PM.

Why I Did It

Each department maintaining its own doc only deepens the fragmentation. Better to build one switchable panel where everyone works off the same data. This switch follows the same logic as the toggle in the progress module below.

Design: Unified pipeline view & multi-dimensional filter panel
3   Progress Toggle ★

Aligning Two Roles in One View

This is the judgment I most want to walk through.
The Conflict I Found

While working on progress visualization, I noticed that PMs ask "how many items are left," while tech leads ask "how many person-days are left." From the same progress data, the two roles want two different answers. Build only one, and the other side has to do the conversion themselves, so miscommunication is inevitable.

What I Did

Instead of building two pages, I added a "by task count / by person-days" toggle. Same underlying data, switch how it's presented, and both roles can align in one view. When progress falls behind, the system flags it in red, and I designed this warning logic as well. The Gantt and burndown charts were an established team decision; I was responsible for their presentation and interaction.

Design: Gantt chart, burndown chart & sprint Kanban

Why This Toggle Matters

Most tools assume everyone seeing the same progress number is enough. But in a 1,000-person development system, managers and tech leads define "progress" differently to begin with. Forcing them onto one number means forcing one side to do the math every time. This toggle isn't an extra chart, it acknowledges that the two roles have inherently different concerns, and resolves it with the lightest possible interaction.

On top of that, this was a system built from scratch, so I aligned key analytics events with the PM up front, such as whether change notifications were actually seen, and how often the filter panel was used. The goal was to ground post-launch iteration in data rather than gut feel.

05 · Upfront Research
Three Weeks Before Any Screen

Before designing anything, I spent three weeks embedded across five departments, sitting in on daily stand-ups and interviewing 15 stakeholders: producers, artists, QA leads, planners, and devops. The point wasn't to collect a feature list, but to understand how a 1,000-person studio actually coordinates day to day, and where it gets stuck.

Every judgment above came out of those three weeks: changes traveling through group chats and getting missed downstream was something I saw repeatedly in stand-ups; PMs confirming progress one lead at a time was a producer's own words; the two roles defining "progress" differently surfaced in the interviews.

06 · What I Learned
01

Build components that snap together, not rigid templates

Game teams work in very different ways, and no single workflow fits everyone. Making states, fields, and views into configurable components lets teams assemble what they need at their own pace, rather than being constrained by the tool. Every time we hard-coded a workflow, a team would soon say "that's not how it works on my side," which is exactly why the three modules could reuse the same interaction.

02

Let the evidence speak

Every design decision I made had a source: a line from an interview, a pattern that kept showing up in stand-ups, an analytics data point. It forced me to swap "I think" for "I saw," and it's why the PM was willing to keep building on my proposals.

03

Sort out the collaboration before touching the interface

The hardest part was never how the interface looks, but thinking through how people coordinate first. The three modules really solve the same thing, bringing misaligned information back into one place. So now, the first thing I do on any project isn't sketching wireframes; it's mapping the existing workflow end to end before I touch the interface. That step has saved me the most rework.

(For similar systems today, I use AI tools to speed up the upfront mapping and prototyping, but the component-reuse, fast-delivery thinking from back then is still the foundation.)
